Case file 01 · Ransomware

WannaCry, Friday 12 May 2017

From the morning of 12 May, computers around the world start showing the same red window. Your files are encrypted. Pay $300 in Bitcoin within three days or the price doubles; after a week the files are gone.

This ransomware doesn't wait for someone to open an attachment. Every infected machine scans its own network and random addresses on the internet for Windows PCs with port 445 (SMB) open, then breaks in with EternalBlue, an NSA exploit that the Shadow Brokers group leaked a month earlier. Microsoft had patched the hole in March. Many organisations hadn't installed the patch.

By the afternoon NHS hospitals in England are cancelling operations and diverting ambulances. Telefónica, Deutsche Bahn, Renault and FedEx report infections too. Nobody knows how to stop it.

Your role. You are Marcus Hutchins, a 22-year-old self-taught malware researcher who blogs as MalwareTech. You're on a week off. You came back from lunch, read the news, and a fellow researcher sent you a sample.

Your mission. Find out what the malware does before it starts encrypting, and use that to stop new infections. Work like a careful analyst: some actions on this machine have consequences.

Case file 01 · Solved

You stopped WannaCry

What really happened

Hutchins noticed that the sample's first act was to request a long nonsense domain that nobody had registered. Registering unknown malware domains and pointing them at a sinkhole is routine work: the sinkhole logs every infected machine that calls in, so researchers can count and map infections. He bought it for $10.69.

Only after the domain went live did he and his colleagues realise what it was. WannaCry asks the domain a question before doing anything. If it gets an answer, it quits. Within minutes, new infections stopped detonating.

Why build a kill switch?

Probably as an anti-analysis trick. Many sandboxes fake a successful reply to every web request. "A nonsense domain answers" can mean "I'm being watched, stay quiet". Because the domain was hard-coded and unregistered, anyone could flip the switch.

What it didn't fix

Machines that were already encrypted stayed encrypted. PCs behind proxies couldn't reach the domain and kept encrypting. Copycat variants with a new kill-switch domain, or none, appeared within days. Researcher Matt Suiche sinkholed one of those too.

The real fix

Install MS17-010, switch off SMBv1, block port 445 at the network edge and keep offline backups. The kill switch only bought organisations the time to do that.

Who was behind it

In December 2017 the US and UK governments publicly attributed WannaCry to the Lazarus Group, linked to North Korea.
